About us

Who we are

A coral restoration program built on science, powered by community.

Our mission

Rescue. Research. Restore.

Reef Response’s mission is to stimulate coral reef ecosystem health through coral restoration, promote community involvement through citizen science, and ensure best practices through scientific research.

Our goals

  1. Increase abundance and diversity

    Grow the number and variety of reef-building corals across the U.S. Virgin Islands.

  2. Involve the community

    Expand community participation in coral reef conservation and research.

  3. Advance the science

    Collect scientific data that improves the efficacy of coral restoration practices.

Citizen science

Citizen scientists helped rebuild our reefs

After Hurricanes Irma and Maria wiped out most of our nursery corals in 2017, Reef Response co-founders Alex Gutting and Kristen Ewen built a citizen science program to help rebuild. Our first community dive, with Red Hook Divers in August 2018, brought together a crew made up entirely of USVI residents who contributed more than 200 person-hours.

Working alongside our scientific divers, citizen scientists cleaned nursery structures, monitored coral growth and health, and collected data that shaped how we restore reefs. Their work let us care for far more corals than our team could have managed alone.

Our in-water volunteer program is paused for now, and we’re working to bring it back. In the meantime, you can still add to the same monitoring effort by reporting what you see on your dives and snorkels.

Field guide

Identifying sick corals

It takes years of training to diagnose coral health reliably — but recognizing a few warning signs and reporting them helps us target disease response and choose future restoration sites.

If you spot any of these on a dive or snorkel, note where you were and submit a coral health report.

Stony coral tissue loss disease

Look for bands or patches of bright white, freshly exposed skeleton right next to healthy tissue, often advancing quickly across a colony. SCTLD reached St. Thomas in 2019 and hits brain, star, and pillar corals especially hard.

Bleaching

Bleached coral turns pale, white, or mottled, but the tissue is still alive — the polyps are intact and the skeleton shows through. It’s a stress response to high water temperatures, and corals can recover if conditions improve.

Predation scars

Small, scattered white patches or bite marks are usually predation, not disease — parrotfish, fireworms, and coral snails all feed on coral tissue. Unlike disease, these scars tend to stay small and don’t spread as an advancing band.

Other diseases & overgrowth

A dark band between healthy tissue and bare white skeleton signals black band disease. Watch too for yellow blotch, dark spots, white plague, and corals being overgrown by boring sponges, tunicates, or anemones.

Founders

Alex Gutting

Co-founder

Alex is one of the co-founders of Reef Response (formerly VI Reef Response), while she was a graduate student at UVI. She and Kristen Ewen led efforts to rebuild the coral nursery at Flat Cay after it was destroyed in the 2017 hurricanes, and created Reef Response’s citizen science program. Alex is now the Coral Conservation Coordinator for The Nature Conservancy in St. Croix, working to strengthen collaborations between the two coral restoration programs.

Kristen Ewen

Co-founder

Kristen was one of the co-founders of Reef Response (formerly VI Reef Response) while at the University of the Virgin Islands. She was integral in rebuilding the Flat Cay nursery following hurricanes Irma and Maria. She, along with Alex Gutting, developed the citizen science component of Reef Response, focusing on including recreational divers in coral nursery maintenance. Kristen is now a Biological Scientist Technician for the National Park Service in St. Croix, USVI.
